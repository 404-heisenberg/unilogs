# User Testing — Round 1

## Methodology

A structured feedback form (Google Forms), distributed to 17 university
students — a mix of the team's own year-groups and people outside the
immediate dev team, so the results reflect more than just people who already
know how UniLogs works. Responses were collected 14–15 September 2026.

Rather than asking testers to rate the finished product in the abstract, the
form asks two things: how they currently track their university work (so the
logbook has something real to be compared against, not a vacuum), and how
important six specific UniLogs features are to them on a 1–5 scale, with a
free-text field for anything they'd change. This is deliberately closer to a
feature-prioritisation survey than a moderated usability test — it tells us
what to build next and what to double down on, which matters for a product
that's still mid-sprint, more than a report on where one tester's cursor
hesitated.

## What testers actually said

A sample of the free-text responses (light copy-editing only, for an
encoding artefact in the original export — content unchanged):

> "Entries currently don't allow me to actually write down my thoughts or
> notes for the entry. I also want stats for each project individually."

> "I'd add reminders or notifications so I don't forget to log things. The
> app is simple and easy to understand."

> "I'd improve the filtering options so I can easily sort entries by subject,
> date and type of activity. The export and sharing features would be
> especially useful."

> "The google suggestions should work for multiple calendars."

> "I'd make the subject templates really flexible because different courses
> need different information. Being able to edit previous entries and quickly
> find old logs would also be useful."

One of these is a discoverability problem rather than a missing feature: free
text in an entry is possible today via a custom `text`-type field on a
project's field definitions, but nothing in the entry-creation flow suggests
that to a first-time user. Worth fixing in the UI, not the data model.

# User Testing — Round 2

## Methodology

Round 1 established _what_ to build, by surveying 17 students on which features
mattered. Round 2 changes method entirely: three **moderated sessions** on the
rebuilt app, run on 28 September 2026, with the screen shared so we could watch
each person decide where to click rather than asking them to rate features in
the abstract.

This is the shift that made Round 2 worth running. A survey tells you a feature
scores badly; a moderated session tells you the participant _hesitated at the
word "aggregation"_, and that is a fix you can actually make. Round 1's
follow-up action — run a second round on the redesigned app — is closed by this
section.

Participants used their own browsers rather than one controlled environment, on
the Sprint 3 build. That means cross-browser differences show up in the findings
instead of being ironed out by a single engine — worth having, given how much of
this sprint touched navigation and layout.

The sessions were exploratory rather than run from a fixed script. Each person
was given a starting task and then left to click through the app in their own
order, with the moderator answering questions but not steering. Six task areas
came up:

| #   | Task area              | What it exercised                                                |
| --- | ---------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1   | Create a project       | Template choice, naming, adding fields, duration, project colour |
| 2   | Add a field            | Adding, editing and removing custom fields, step progression     |
| 3   | Create an entry        | Field inputs, the right-hand panel, tags, the save/preview step  |
| 4   | Navigate the app       | Rail icons, folder tree, dashboard widgets, account vs settings  |
| 5   | Read a project's stats | Summary cards, field insights, the aggregation controls          |
| 6   | Find and share entries | Search, filters, project view, and what a share link exposes     |

**Difference from Round 1, stated plainly.** Round 1 produced charts because
17 survey responses quantify cleanly. Round 2 has three participants, so it
produces **no quantitative charts and no ratings** — the evidence here is
per-task outcome and quoted behaviour. A three-person sample cannot support a
statistic, and presenting one anyway would misrepresent it. What it can support
is finding a problem three unrelated people hit the same way, which is what
happened here: two things defeated all three participants independently —
understanding what a project's **fields** are, and noticing the **right-hand
panel** on the entry editor.

**Limitations, stated honestly.** The sessions were not recorded, so the quotes
below are the moderator's contemporaneous written notes. They are verbatim in
wording but have lost the surrounding conversation, and are lightly
copy-edited for spelling only — the same treatment Round 1's export got. Year
of study and prior logbook use were not collected, so Round 2's participants
cannot be compared against Round 1's respondent profile. Findings are reported
individually rather than counted: three people failing at a screen is three
observations, not "3/3 users", and should be read as _we have now seen this
three times_, not as a prevalence figure.

### What surprised the moderator

Three things did not match our expectations going in:

1. **The `UL` monogram split the room.** Luthando read it as decorative and
   inert, and wanted it out of the navbar; Krisha assumed it was a link and
   clicked it. An element that two users interpret in opposite ways is worse
   than one they both ignore, because neither will ask. Both then converged on
   the same replacement — their own **initials** (`LM`, `KM`) at the foot of the
   page, directly above the settings wheel, leading to account settings. An
   initials avatar next to the settings gear is a recognisable convention;
   a gold `UL` monogram in the navbar is not, and means nothing to anyone who
   has not already seen the product.
2. **The `Save` step failed as a concept, not a control.** Jared and Luthando
   both, unprompted, felt it was out of place. Neither realised it was showing
   a _preview_ of the project before it was saved and before entries could be
   created. A correctly-functioning button that reads as the wrong action.
3. **The core premise did not survive contact.** Jared said the idea that
   entries feed statistics, insights and a growing logbook simply "did not
   land", and Krisha independently described the intended purpose of field
   insights as one the app is not currently meeting. This is a product-positioning
   gap, not a bug, and it is the most consequential thing Round 2 produced.

## What testers actually said

Verbatim from the moderator's session notes (spelling lightly corrected):

> "all the notes will have in common"

— Jared, describing what the entry filters do. Useful, because it is the mental
model a first-time user brings, and it is not the one the UI teaches.

> "blank meant completely empty"

— Krisha, on the `Blank` template. She was confused about why she should not
simply continue to the next step, and suggested renaming it to `Custom`.

> "I couldn't scroll up while carrying a widget."

— Krisha, on the dashboard. This is why moving a widget to the top meant
dragging it past every other widget in turn.

> "things in the navbar are expected to be clickable and it isn't"

— Luthando, on the `UL` monogram. An item in the navbar that cannot be clicked
is read as broken, so he wanted it out of the navbar altogether — replaced by his
own **initials** at the foot of the page, sitting just above the settings wheel,
with the click target being account settings. Krisha independently asked for the
same change (`UL` → `KM`).

> "we shouldn't say that we have email remainders if we don't implement or plan
> on implementing it"

— Luthando, on the reminder settings. He wanted in-app and email reminders
stated separately, because the current wording left him believing email
reminders did not exist at all.

> "we changed what add means"

— Luthando, on the `Add` button during project creation. He believed it was
meant to save the current field and continue to a new one; instead it saved the
field and then asked him to create another one, making it a two-click process.

> "a guided walkthrough that other apps provide would work really well for our
> app"

— Luthando, unprompted. Worth flagging that our tutor raised the same thing in
project meetings, which makes it the one Round 2 finding with independent
corroboration from outside the sessions.

### What these five fixes did not cover

Being precise about this matters more than the fixes themselves:

- Renaming `Blank` to `Custom` does **not** answer Krisha's other point — that
  she knew they were templates but not what they were for or how they affected
  her project. There is still no explanation of what fields _are_, at the point
  of creation. That was all three participants' single largest problem, and it
  is untouched.
- Two participants asked for the `UL` monogram to become their **own initials**
  (`LM`, `KM`) at the foot of the page, above the settings wheel, linking to
  account settings. We made it a working link instead. That answers Krisha's
  belief that it was already clickable, but not Luthando's objection that a
  non-clickable item has no business sitting in the navbar. The initials change
  is cheap, and two people asked for it independently.
- Jared's person icon for account settings, and his dashboard-icon confusion,
  are untouched. #304 separates the _sections_; the rail _icons_ are unchanged.
- Live reminder state is now shown, but the in-app versus email conflation
  Luthando identified is not resolved — the settings still do not clearly
  separate the two.
- The summary-card hierarchy change addresses legibility of the values. It does
  not address Krisha's "bland and white" nor Luthando's "information overload".

One finding is worth calling out because of _how_ it surfaced. Moving sign out
into the Account section broke the two e2e error-path specs, which reach the
button by role and accessible name. Labelling the monogram `Your account
settings` separately collided with an existing `getByRole('link', { name:
'Settings' })` locator, because Playwright matches accessible names as
case-insensitive substrings. Neither was a product defect — both were the test
layer correctly refusing to let a refactor pass unnoticed, and the second is a
concrete argument for keeping the e2e suite thin but real.

### What Round 2 actually told us

The five shipped fixes are real but they are the small end of this. The
consistent signal across all three sessions is that UniLogs asks a first-time
user to understand a three-level model — project, fields, entries — and gives
them no explanation of it before asking them to act. Two participants could not
tell they were filling in fields; two could not find the panel that holds them.

The second signal is that the product's reason for existing is not visible in
the interface. Entry insights were described from the outside as bland, and
from the inside as not doing their job. Jared's reading — that entries feeding
statistics, insights and a growing logbook did not land — is a positioning
problem, and no amount of tooltip copy fixes it.

The third is that unlabelled icons and unexplained jargon are not cosmetic. They
are where the sessions were lost, repeatedly.
