# Project Methodology

Team Code of Duty runs this project using **Scrum**, an Agile framework defined by
Schwaber and Sutherland in *The Scrum Guide* (2020).

This page states what the framework requires, what we actually do, and — where the
two differ — why we deviated. The deviations are deliberate and recorded rather
than accidental.

## Why Agile, and why Scrum

The brief for UniLogs is not fixed. Requirements are tiered basic, intermediate
and advanced, and the client is a tutor we meet weekly rather than a specification
handed over once. Feature detail arrives progressively over ten weeks.

A plan-driven approach such as Waterfall assumes requirements are known up front
and stable. Ours are neither. Committing to a full specification in week one would
mean either building the wrong thing or renegotiating the plan every time the
client clarified something.

Agile suits this because it plans in short increments and expects requirements to
change. Within Agile we chose Scrum over Kanban for one specific reason: **the
course imposes four hard deadlines.** Scrum's fixed-length sprints map directly
onto those deadlines, and each sprint ends in something demonstrable. Kanban's
continuous flow has no equivalent forcing function, and with seven students
balancing other courses, a deadline that arrives on a known date is more useful
than one that does not.

## Roles

| Scrum role | Who | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Product Owner | Our tutor-client | Sets and prioritises requirements, signs off on what is done |
| Scrum Master | Mosey | Runs ceremonies and removes blockers |
| Development Team | All seven members | Everyone writes code, including the Scrum Master |

**The Scrum Master does not assign work.** This is worth stating plainly because
it is the most commonly misunderstood part of the role. The Scrum Master's job is
to run ceremonies, remove impediments and protect the process. Deciding who builds
what is the development team's own responsibility.

Work is **pulled, not pushed.** A team member finishes an issue, returns to the
board, and takes the next one they can do. Nobody waits to be told.

## Sprints

Our sprints are defined by the course milestone deadlines.

| Sprint | Ends | Focus |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sprint 1 | 25 August 2026 | Project setup, design, development plan, first features |
| Sprint 2 | 15 September 2026 | Core features, testing, CI/CD, deployment |
| Sprint 3 | 29 September 2026 | Intermediate features, external API integration |
| Sprint 4 | 11 October 2026 | Advanced features, polish, final documentation |

Each sprint is tracked as a **GitHub Milestone**, with every issue assigned to
exactly one. The milestone due date is the sprint end date, which gives us a
burn-down of remaining work without maintaining a separate tracker.

## Ceremonies

### Sprint Planning

At the start of each sprint the team reviews the product backlog, selects the
items that fit the available time, and breaks them into issues on the board. Each
issue is small enough for one person to complete and carries acceptance criteria.

Prioritisation follows the requirement tiers agreed with the client: basic
requirements are completed before intermediate ones, intermediate before advanced.

### Daily Scrum

We hold standups over Discord voice, in the `#meetings` channel. Each member covers
what they did, what they are doing next, and what is blocking them.

**Our deviation from the Scrum Guide is frequency, not format.** The Guide
specifies a 15-minute meeting every day at the same time. Seven students with
different timetables and course loads cannot hold a daily meeting that everyone
reliably attends, and a ceremony half the team skips is worse than one honestly
scheduled less often. We hold standups several times per sprint, and members raise
blockers in Discord between meetings rather than waiting for the next one.

Each standup is recorded in `#meetings` with the date, who attended, and a written
summary of what was discussed and what was agreed.

### Sprint Review

At the end of each sprint we demonstrate the working increment to our tutor-client
and they decide which backlog items are genuinely done. An item is only done when
it is code complete, merged to `main`, deployed, and tested.

Feedback from the review feeds directly into the next sprint's backlog.

### Sprint Retrospective

After each review the team meets without the client to discuss what went well,
what did not, and what we will change. Retrospectives are written up and committed
to the repository.

Retrospectives are explicitly blame-free. The purpose is to improve the process,
not to attribute fault.

### Backlog Refinement

Ongoing rather than scheduled. Large items are broken down as they approach the
top of the backlog, estimates are adjusted as we learn how long things actually
take, and items that are no longer relevant are removed.

## Artefacts

| Scrum artefact | Our implementation |
| --- | --- |
| Product Backlog | GitHub Projects board, Todo column |
| Sprint Backlog | Issues assigned to the current GitHub Milestone |
| Increment | The deployed application at the end of each sprint |

The board has columns for Todo, In Progress, In Review and Done, plus filtered
views per sub-team. An issue only reaches Done when its pull request is merged and
the feature is demonstrable.

## Deviations from the Scrum Guide

Recorded openly, with reasoning.

| Deviation | Why |
| --- | --- |
| Standups several times per sprint rather than daily | Seven students with incompatible timetables. A less frequent meeting everyone attends is worth more than a daily one half the team misses. Blockers are raised in Discord between meetings. |
| Uneven sprint lengths | Sprint boundaries are set by course deadlines, not by us. Sprint 1 runs longer than Sprint 4. The Scrum Guide prefers consistent length; we cannot control this. |
| The Product Owner is our tutor-client, not a team member | Follows the course structure. It does mean the Product Owner is less available than Scrum assumes, so we prepare specific written questions for each meeting rather than relying on ad-hoc access. |
| No formal story point estimation | With seven people new to the stack, early estimates would be close to guesswork. We size issues qualitatively — small enough for one person, one branch, one pull request — and refine as we learn our actual velocity. |

Documenting an adaptation and its reasoning is more honest than claiming to run
textbook Scrum while doing something else.
